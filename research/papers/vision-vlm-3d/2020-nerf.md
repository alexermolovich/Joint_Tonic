## Core paper explanation

NeRF represents a scene with a neural function that predicts density and view-dependent color at 3D locations. A camera pixel defines a ray; sampling the field along that ray and accumulating light produces its color. Training adjusts the field so rendered images agree with observed images from known camera poses. The useful implementation boundary is the rendering computation, which can be tested before any neural network is trained.

## Execution flow

Camera geometry creates world-frame rays, a radiance field predicts samples along each ray, and volume compositing produces pixels. Training differentiates rendered-pixel error back into the field.

### Construct camera rays

For pixel $(u,v)$, intrinsics $K$, camera-to-world rotation $R_{wc}$, and world-frame camera center $o_w$, choose

$$
d_w=\operatorname{normalize}\left(R_{wc}K^{-1}[u,v,1]^\top\right),
\qquad r(t)=o_w+t d_w.
$$

With a unit direction, $t$ is distance in the scene’s length units. [Project Camera Points into Pixels](/problems/project-camera-points) implements the corresponding forward pinhole relation; [SE(3) transforms](/problems/se3-transform-points) help keep camera/world conventions consistent. NeRF camera datasets can use different axis conventions from the optical frame in those exercises, so conversion must be explicit.


**Prerequisite practice:** [Apply an SE(3) Transform to Batched Points](/problems/se3-transform-points) · [Project Camera Points into Pixels](/problems/project-camera-points). These supporting skills do not complete this model stage.


**New runnable component:** [Stratify Samples Along Near and Far Bounds](/problems/nerf-stratified-samples). The exercise implements its stated bounded component with original synthetic fixtures.

### Query the radiance field

The neural field returns density $\sigma_i\ge0$ and RGB color $c_i\in\mathbb R^3$ at sample $x_i=r(t_i)$. Density has inverse-length units. Positional encoding expands a scalar coordinate with sinusoidal frequencies, for example

$$
\gamma(x)=(\sin(2^0\pi x),\cos(2^0\pi x),\ldots,
\sin(2^{L-1}\pi x),\cos(2^{L-1}\pi x)).
$$

Apply the encoding to each spatial coordinate after the chosen scene normalization. The density branch is position-dependent; the color branch also receives viewing direction, allowing appearance to vary with viewpoint.


**Implement this component:** [Encode 3D Positions with Fourier Features](/problems/nerf-fourier-features). The positional encoder uses its explicit channel order and excludes raw coordinates.

### Composite and train

For ordered intervals $\Delta_i=t_{i+1}-t_i$, define opacity and transmittance:

$$
\alpha_i=1-e^{-\sigma_i\Delta_i},\qquad
T_i=\exp\left(-\sum_{j<i}\sigma_j\Delta_j\right),
\qquad \hat C(r)=\sum_iT_i\alpha_i c_i.
$$

The factors $\sigma_i\Delta_i$ are dimensionless. Samples behind an opaque region receive almost no weight. A background term can carry remaining transmittance. Training minimizes squared rendered-color error on sampled rays:

$$
\mathcal L=\sum_{r\in\mathcal R}\|\hat C(r)-C^*(r)\|_2^2,
$$

with separate coarse/fine predictions in the original hierarchical-sampling method. Multiplying by interval length again after forming $\alpha_i$ would count it twice.


**Implement this component:** [Compute Transmittance and Alpha Compositing Weights](/problems/nerf-alpha-compositing). The compositor uses supplied finite metric intervals and returns explicit background mass; it does not train or query the radiance field.


**New runnable component:** [Render Color and Expected Depth Along a Ray](/problems/nerf-render-color-depth). This returns conditional foreground depth and explicit validity; it does not assume a depth for the background.

**Planned component problems:** Detect Bad Poses with Reprojection and Render Residuals; Differentiate Rendered Color on a Small Synthetic Field. These are not runnable; titles without reviewed blueprints remain unreviewed ideas.

## Why this matters on a robot

A radiance field connects perception to calibrated multi-view geometry, but a sharp rendering is not automatically an accurate collision surface. Pose error, moving objects, unobserved regions, and view-dependent effects can all produce misleading geometry. Keep render quality, depth accuracy, and planning suitability as separate evaluation targets.
